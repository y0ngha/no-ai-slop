# A reservation trial for the shared machines

To: Alder Street Workshop steering group
From: Nora Bell, evening coordinator
Date: October 6
Decision requested: authorize a limited eight-week trial, subject to the staffing condition below

## Recommendation

I recommend trying a shared reservation calendar for the table saw, laser cutter, and large sewing machine for eight weeks. The trial would cover staffed member sessions on Tuesday and Thursday evenings and Saturday afternoons. Classes, open repair mornings, and the pottery room would keep their present arrangements. We have enough evidence of friction to try something, but not enough to select a permanent system.

The immediate decision is whether to make time available for the trial, including the work of keeping it usable. I am asking for a maximum of $180 for calendar hosting and printed materials, and two coordinator hours each week. If we cannot identify those hours before October 20, we should delay the start. A calendar that everyone assumes somebody else is maintaining will give us a new place to have the same argument.

## What is happening now

At present, members put their names on a whiteboard after they arrive. It is simple and generally works when the room is quiet. On a busy evening it rewards arriving early, which is easier for some members than others. People also stand near a machine to make their interest visible. The queue is partly on the board and partly in a conversation that a newcomer may not know is happening.

Across 12 staffed sessions in September, volunteers recorded 19 occasions when somebody waited more than 30 minutes for one of the three machines. These were observed occasions, not 19 distinct people. In four cases the person left before using the equipment. We did not record how often the machines sat idle, so this is not an equipment utilization study. It tells us that waiting can be frustrating. It does not tell us whether advance booking will increase the amount of useful work completed.

Our workshop has 74 active members, of whom 28 used at least one of these machines during those sessions. Those counts exclude class participants. We should be careful about letting the most vocal machine users define convenience for everyone else. At the same time, the members carrying wood across town have a reasonable interest in knowing whether they can cut it when they arrive.

## The proposed arrangement

Half of the available machine time would be bookable seven days ahead, in 45-minute blocks. The remaining half would stay available for arrival-day use. A member could hold one advance booking per machine per week. Unused bookings would be released after ten minutes, provided the duty volunteer has checked that the member is absent rather than collecting material from the store cupboard.

The calendar would show a first name and member number, with a printed sheet beside the whiteboard. Members could ask the coordinator to enter a booking by phone or in person. We would not require a smartphone. This matters more than it may sound: two regular Saturday members share a household computer, and one has told me she will simply stop trying if access depends on watching a booking page.

The 45 minutes include setup and clearing away. A reservation would not confer permission to use equipment without the existing induction. I should have put that earlier because several people have already understood “reserve” to mean “approved to operate.” The duty volunteer would still make that check. The calendar would only allocate time; it would not carry the safety record or decide whether a proposed job is suitable.

## People who need different things

Lewis, who makes cabinets after his shift, wants the certainty of a slot. He said, “I can carry the boards here or I can gamble on the queue, but doing both every Thursday is getting old.” His journey is part of the cost even though it does not appear in our session records. He favors booking all the time in advance, which I do not support for this trial.

Mara runs the informal mending circle and worries about losing the spontaneous help that happens around the sewing machine. A person often arrives needing five minutes and leaves having learned something from another member. Breaking that into appointments could change the atmosphere. She is willing to try the half-booked arrangement if volunteers can still fit a genuinely short task between reservations without taking time from the next person.

The duty volunteers have a different concern. Six of our nine volunteers responded to the proposal, and four expect enforcement to become another difficult conversation at closing time. They want a visible rule for overruns. The proposed rule is to stop at the end of the slot, except where stopping immediately would leave an unsafe operation. This exception cannot become a general extension for a project that is almost finished.

## Exceptions and responsibility

Equipment faults would cancel the affected reservations. We would offer a replacement slot where one exists, but no priority claim on the following week's entire calendar. A class running late would not take booked member time; the tutor would have to finish with another activity. This will need agreement from the two tutors who share the laser cutter, and neither has yet reviewed the detailed rule.

Accessibility adjustments may require a longer setup period. The coordinator could arrange a double block after discussing what time is needed, without asking the member to explain their circumstances on the public sheet. That would count as their weekly advance booking. We have not established whether two blocks will be enough in every case, and should leave room to revise the arrangement with the person concerned.

Responsibility for exception handling needs a level of procedural clarity that avoids informal inequity. In ordinary terms, a friend of the volunteer should not get a longer turn just because asking feels easy. The coordinator would keep a brief private log of exceptions, including the reason and minutes involved. We would review patterns, rather than circulate a list of who asked for help.

## What would count as useful

For the trial we would record waits over 30 minutes, missed bookings, overruns, and minutes spent administering the calendar. We would also ask members whether they could complete the task they came to do. Completion will be self-reported and the tasks will vary. I do not propose converting that answer into a productivity score.

At week four, we would check whether the administration is staying within two hours a week and whether arrival-day users are regularly being turned away. At week eight, the steering group would decide whether to continue, change the balance of bookable time, or stop. Fewer waits would be encouraging, but fewer waits achieved by discouraging occasional members would not be a satisfactory outcome. We need the counts and the comments together.

## Conditions before we start

Rafi has offered to set up the calendar but has not offered to run it indefinitely. I can handle the first two weeks. We still need a named person for the remaining six, tutor agreement on class overruns, and a paper booking process tested during one ordinary session. If those pieces are ready by October 20, we can begin on October 27. Otherwise I recommend postponement, with a revised date brought back to the group.

I am in favor of the trial because the current queue already allocates advantages, just quietly. I am also attached to the workshop being a place where you can arrive with an unfinished idea. Half the time left open is an imperfect way of protecting that. We should try it only if we are willing to admit that a tidier calendar can still make a worse afternoon.
