# Label study: findings and proposed next step

The purpose of this round was to develop a more informed understanding of how people interpret the two labels used in the Cedar scheduling prototype. This was a small study intended to support a near-term product decision, and the findings should be considered within that frame. There were some useful differences in performance, alongside comments that did not all point in the same direction. Having considered both, we recommend taking the label "Save for later" into the next prototype and keeping "Submit request" for the action that sends a request to an approver.

We tested two alternatives for the action that stores an unfinished request without sending it. Version A called it "Save draft"; Version B called it "Save for later". The rest of the screen, including the button placement and explanatory text, was identical. The prototype did not send real requests. We wanted to see whether participants could preserve their work and whether they understood that an approver would not yet receive anything. Those are related questions, although succeeding at one does not necessarily establish success at the other.

Twelve people participated in moderated sessions on 14 and 15 September 2026. All arranged shifts at least once a month, and none had used Cedar before. Six saw Version A first and six saw Version B first. Each person completed one unfinished-request task in each version, using different names and dates. This arrangement reduced a simple order imbalance but did not remove the possibility that the first task taught people how the second task worked. It is an important limitation in the interpretation of the comparison.

On first exposure, five of the six participants who saw Version B chose the intended action without a moderator prompt. Three of the six who saw Version A did so. These are observations from very small groups, not a dependable estimate of a future conversion difference. Across both exposures, nine participants correctly explained that saving would not notify an approver in Version B; seven did so in Version A. We recorded understanding from their explanations, rather than treating the button click itself as proof. One participant selected the intended action in both versions but still believed a manager would be alerted.

The comments provide a little additional context. Four participants associated "draft" with email and understood it immediately. Three others described it as a technical term or said they did not usually see it in scheduling tools. "Save for later" felt more familiar to several people, but two asked whether it meant the system would submit the request automatically later. That ambiguity is a meaningful concern. The label performed better on our immediate task, while also creating a possible expectation that needs to be addressed in the surrounding copy.

In terms of next steps, the proposed decision is to use "Save for later" with the sentence "Only you can see this until you submit it." underneath the saved-request confirmation. This is a prototype decision, not approval to release the wording to every customer. We will retain "Submit request" as the separate sending action and will not add an automatic submission feature. A second round with eight new participants should check the automatic-submission misunderstanding explicitly before the release review on 6 October 2026.

There are further boundaries to what this work tells us. Sessions were conducted in English on desktop screens. We did not test translated labels, mobile layouts, or people using screen readers. The prototype also omitted the pressure of an actual shift deadline. Product leads can use these findings to choose the next version to examine, but should avoid reporting that the study proved an overall usability improvement. The immediate decision is reasonably supported; the wider claim remains untested.
