# The cupboard by the back gate

When our building put a tool cupboard beside the back gate, I thought it would save us buying tools we could share. There are fourteen flats here, and it seemed unlikely that we all needed our own long spirit level. I liked the idea. Then, for nearly a month, I walked past the cupboard without opening it, even when a loose shelf at home gave me a reason to do so.

Borrowing from a shop has an established procedure. Borrowing from a neighbour involves imagining what they might think of you. I worried about returning a drill with a scratch that had already been there. I also worried about being the person who kept something for too long. Small worries, but enough to leave the shelf leaning.

Eventually I met Ruth from the ground floor at the gate and asked about the drill. She opened the cupboard and showed me the paper notebook hanging inside the door. "Name, flat, tool, return day," she said. The drill already had plenty of scratches. There was masking tape on its case that read "Battery sticks. Pull straight." Somehow that instruction made the whole arrangement feel more approachable. It was an object with a history, and a slightly inconvenient battery, rather than a test of my character.

I borrowed it on Saturday and wrote down Sunday as my return day. The shelf took twenty minutes. Cleaning the dust out of the case took another five, which I mention because I had not initially regarded that as part of borrowing. On Sunday morning I returned it and crossed out my entry. Nothing memorable happened, and that was rather a relief.

Since then, I have added my small socket set to the cupboard. I put it in a blue tin and wrote my flat number underneath. Someone used it last week and left a note saying the smallest socket was missing before they started. They were right; it had been in my kitchen drawer for months. I was glad they told me. A missing piece was easier to sort out than the polite assumption that somebody else had noticed.

We now have a simple rule that loans last no more than three days unless you arrange a longer loan with the owner. I used to think rules might make a neighbourly project feel unfriendly. In practice, this one gives me less to guess about. I still check the notebook twice when I borrow something, but I do borrow things. The shelf is straight, the socket is back in its tin, and I know Ruth a little better than I did before.
